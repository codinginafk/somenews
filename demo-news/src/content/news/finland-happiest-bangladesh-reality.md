**Short answer:** The claim is two errors from one table. The World Happiness Report ranks countries on six inputs, not on whether a country is good, and its bottom of the table is not a measurement of being the worst place to live. Finland is top because it scores at the top of most of the six inputs, including the two that move national scores the most: income per head and perceived corruption. Bangladesh scores low on those same two. Neither position is a verdict on the people who live there, and the report says so itself.

## 1. What the ranking is built from

The World Happiness Report does not ask a simple question and rank the answers. It assembles a country score from six inputs: GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy at birth, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption.

Three of those inputs are survey-derived and three are economic or demographic. The report's method notes and its country table are published for every edition, and the current one is the 2026 report.

The consequence is that the ranking is partly a ranking of income per head and partly a ranking of institutional trust, with a life-expectancy term and three soft inputs around them. Finland sits at the top because it is near the top of most of those columns. That is a real and remarkable result. It is also a specific set of columns.

## 2. Why Finland is at the top

Two mechanisms do most of the work, and both are worth naming.

The first is that Finland's score is not one number but a stack. High income per head, high social support, high healthy life expectancy and low perceived corruption all point the same way, so a country near the top of each is placed near the top of the total. This is why the top of the table looks like a list of countries with functioning institutions rather than a list of pleasant places.

The second is that the survey component measures life evaluation, which respondents answer as a judgement on their life overall. A country with strong social safety nets and low perceived corruption produces high life-evaluation answers for reasons that are structural rather than seasonal.

## 3. Why Bangladesh is near the bottom, and what that does not mean

Bangladesh's position is driven by the same two inputs that put Finland on top, running the other way. Lower income per head and weaker institutional trust scores pull the total down.

What that does not include is any measure of the daily texture of life: whether people eat with extended family, whether a bus commute works, whether flood water is cleared quickly, whether a small business hires every month. Those things are real, they are not in the six columns, and a rank that says "low" does not speak to them at all.

The methodological point generalises. Our [Dhaka piece](/dhaka-beyond-rank/) works through how a national cell flattens a city, and our [safety maps brief](/safest-country-maps-footnote/) does the same job for crime measures.

## 4. The ranking next door: safety

Life evaluation and safety are not the same measurement, and it is worth reading the second one properly.

Gallup's Law and Order Index uses four questions: whether you feel safe walking alone at night, whether you have confidence in the local police, whether money or property was stolen from you in the last 12 months, and whether you were assaulted or mugged in the past 12 months. It is built on nationally representative probability samples of about 1,000 or more adults per country across 144 countries and territories, with a margin of error of plus or minus 3.3 to plus or minus 5.4 percentage points. The world score is 83 out of 100.

The list is not a Nordic monopoly. Tajikistan scores 97, Singapore 95, Kosovo 94, and China, Iceland and Vietnam each 93. In the Middle East and North Africa, Bahrain, Oman and Saudi Arabia each score 91. Two different instruments, two different questions, and they do not produce the same order.

## 5. The verdict: missing context

The claim is false in both directions. Finland is not the best country in the world, and Bangladesh is not the worst. They are top and bottom of one table that measures six specific things, and a table is not a verdict.

The context that makes the ranking worth reading is that it is unusually transparent. The report publishes its inputs, its method notes and its country table, and Gallup publishes the exact wording of its questions alongside its sampling frame and margin of error. A ranking that shows its working can be checked, argued with and used properly, which is more than most viral map data ever allows.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Is Finland really the happiest country?**
A: It is top of the World Happiness Report's ranking, which measures six inputs including income per head and perceived corruption. That is a different statement from "the best country in the world".

**Q: Does a low rank mean people are unhappy?**
A: It means the country scores low on the six inputs used, two of which are income and institutional trust. It says nothing about the parts of daily life the index leaves out.

**Q: Why does the happiness ranking and the safety ranking disagree?**
A: They ask different questions. One asks for a judgement on life overall, the other asks four safety-specific questions. A country can top one and sit mid-table on the other.
