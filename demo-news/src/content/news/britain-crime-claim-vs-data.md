**Short answer:** A viral map that puts Britain at the top of a rape league table is not measuring rape. It is measuring how many cases police recorded, in countries that define, count and report the offence differently. The UK does record a large number of sexual offences. The latest ONS release puts the figure at 209,079 for England and Wales. But the same release shows that only about one in seven victims of rape or assault by penetration reported the most recent assault to police. A count built on the minority who report cannot rank countries by how much rape occurs.

## 1. What the viral post claims

The viral artefact is usually a screenshot. It shows a bar chart or a colour-coded map with the UK near the top and a headline calling Britain the "rape capital of the world." There is often no source link, no year and no definition of the offence being counted.

The claim has a surface logic. Police in England and Wales recorded 209,079 sexual offences in the year ending March 2025, an increase of 11% on the previous year, according to the ONS. That is a large number. If you put large numbers from different countries side by side, the UK will often look high.

The problem is what the number represents. A police-recorded offence is a case that a victim reported and an officer recorded under a particular legal category. It is not a count of every rape that happened.

## 2. What the UK data actually shows

The ONS publishes two measures that answer different questions. Police recorded crime counts offences that were reported and recorded. The Crime Survey for England and Wales asks a sample of the public what they experienced, whether or not they went to police.

The police figure for the year ending March 2025 was 209,079 sexual offences. The ONS says over half of the 11% increase came from two new sexual offences introduced by the Online Safety Act 2023. It also warns that police-recorded sexual offences "do not provide a reliable measure of trends," because increases can reflect better recording practices and changes in victim reporting.

The survey gives a different picture. The CSEW estimated that about 900,000 people aged 16 and over experienced sexual assault in the year ending March 2025. That is 1.9% of the age group: 3.0% of females and 0.7% of males. It also estimated that 15.9% of people aged 16 and over, about 7.7 million, had experienced sexual assault since the age of 16. Of those, 1.9 million, or 4.0%, had experienced rape including attempts. That breaks down as 7.3% of females and 0.5% of males.

Two further details matter for the viral claim. First, 54.7% of people who had experienced rape or assault by penetration since 16 said the most recent assault was committed by a partner or ex-partner. Second, only 14.7% reported that most recent assault to the police.

That last figure is the heart of the problem. If roughly one in seven victims reports, then police data captures a small and uneven slice of the total. The slice depends on who feels able to report, which depends on trust, stigma, support services and the legal system.

A note on geography: the ONS data covers England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate justice systems and separate statistics. "Britain" in the viral posts usually means the whole UK, which makes the comparison even looser.

## 3. Why police counts cannot be a league table

The UNODC has set out why cross-country crime comparisons are hard. It lists three reasons: different legal definitions, different levels of reporting and policing, and different social, economic and political contexts.

The definition problem is immediate. One country's "rape" may require proof of penetration. Another may record it as "sexual assault" or "assault by penetration." Age thresholds differ. Marital rape is treated differently across jurisdictions. Some systems count each incident separately; others count a course of conduct as one case. A chart that adds these categories together is not comparing like with like.

The reporting problem is just as serious. The UNODC notes that reporting levels depend on access to police, trust in institutions and social norms. In societies where women are discouraged from reporting sexual violence, the recorded count will be low even if the true rate is high. In societies with strong victim support and broad recording rules, the count will be high even if the true rate is not.

The UNODC's conclusion is blunt. "In the case of some categories of violent crime - such as rape or assault - country to country comparisons may simply be unreliable and misleading."

That does not mean the UK figure is fake. It means the figure is a measure of reported and recorded cases, not a global ranking of prevalence.

## 4. What victim surveys add

Surveys are not perfect either. They rely on samples, recall and what people are willing to disclose. But they answer a different question. The CSEW is designed to estimate prevalence across the population, including people who never contact police.

The ONS says the CSEW provides reliable estimates using a consistent methodology that is not affected by changes in recording practices, new offences or changes in the propensity to report. That is why it is the better measure of whether sexual violence is rising or falling.

The latest CSEW estimates show that sexual assault is common. About 900,000 people experienced it in the last year. About 7.7 million had experienced it since the age of 16. Those are not small numbers, and they are not a reason to dismiss the issue.

But the survey cannot produce a global league table either. Other countries run different surveys with different questions, different age ranges and different definitions. A UK survey result and a survey result from another country are not directly comparable unless the methodology is harmonised. The UNODC makes the same point about police data, and it applies to surveys too.

## 5. What the claim gets right

The viral posts are not wrong that sexual violence in the UK is serious. The CSEW estimates that 1.9 million people have experienced rape including attempts since the age of 16. More than half of rape and penetration victims were assaulted by a partner or ex-partner. Those figures describe a real and widespread harm.

The posts are also right that police-recorded sexual offences have risen. The ONS recorded 209,079 in the year ending March 2025, up 11%. Some of that rise reflects new offences and better recording, but the underlying volume is real.

Where the claim goes wrong is the leap from "high recorded count" to "highest prevalence in the world." A high count can mean more crime, but it can also mean more reporting, broader definitions and better recording. The viral map does not tell you which.

There is a legitimate debate about how well the UK justice system responds to sexual violence. Conviction rates, court delays and victim support are all worth scrutinising. A misleading league table does not help that debate. It replaces a hard question about institutions with a simple ranking that the data cannot support.

## 6. How to read a crime ranking

Before sharing a chart that ranks countries on rape, check five things.

First, check the jurisdiction. England and Wales are not the UK, and the UK is not a single legal system.

Second, check the source and year. A chart without a source link cannot be verified.

Third, check the definition. "Rape," "sexual assault" and "sexual offences" are different categories. A chart that mixes them is not comparing one crime.

Fourth, check whether the data is police-recorded or survey-based. Police data reflects reporting and recording. Survey data reflects what people say they experienced. They answer different questions.

Fifth, check the denominator. A raw count will make populous countries look worse. A rate per 100,000 people is fairer, but it still inherits the definition and reporting problems.

If a ranking mixes police data across countries without explaining its methodology, treat it as misleading. ## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Is Britain the rape capital of the world?**
A: No credible dataset supports that ranking. Police-recorded rape counts differ by legal definition, recording rules and reporting rates. The UNODC says such comparisons may be unreliable and misleading.

**Q: Why does the UK record so many rapes?**
A: Police recording has improved, the offence categories are broad, and more victims may be willing to report than in some other countries. The ONS says recorded sexual offences are not a reliable measure of trends.

**Q: What share of victims report to the police?**
A: The CSEW estimates that 14.7% of people who experienced rape or assault by penetration since the age of 16 reported the most recent assault to police, for the year ending March 2025.

**Q: What is the best UK measure of sexual violence?**
A: The Crime Survey for England and Wales, because it includes crimes that are not reported to police and uses a consistent methodology. It estimated that 1.9% of people aged 16 and over experienced sexual assault in the year ending March 2025.
