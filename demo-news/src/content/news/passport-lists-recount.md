**Short answer:** "Passport falls three places" is almost never a change in the rules. It is usually a change in counting, a different data vintage, or a category that one index bundles and another splits. A passport holder's actual access is governed by the border rules, and those rules did not move. The rank did. The fix is four questions, and the reason they matter is that visa agents and airlines act on the graphic while embassies act on the treaty.

## 1. Two indexes, two rules for the same travel access

The Henley Passport Index and the Global Citizen Solutions passport index both publish a single score per passport, and they score the same underlying reality differently.

The Henley index counts destinations where a passport grants visa-free access or entry on arrival, and it publishes the methodology and the date of each data refresh. The Global Citizen Solutions index publishes its own passport ranking and, importantly for a fact-checker, an archive of earlier editions.

The arithmetic is the same in both cases and it is worth doing once. Take the number of destinations a passport opens up, subtract the ones where a visa is required, and you have the access count. Two publishers using the same definition can still disagree, because the definitions differ at the edges: what counts as visa-on-arrival versus eTA, whether an electronic authorisation is bundled with visa-free access or split out, and how long a stay each status allows.

When two indexes disagree by a few places, the disagreement is usually one of those edges, not a fact about the passport.

## 2. Three ways a rank moves without the rules moving

The first is a category change. When a destination introduces an electronic travel authorisation, one index may add it to the access pot straight away and another may pause the count until the first approvals clear. The rank gap opens for a fortnight and closes. No treaty changed for any holder.

The second is a data refresh. Indexes update on their own cycles, and they publish the refresh date at the foot of the table. Two graphics circulating in the same week can be built on different vintages of the same underlying data.

The third is a genuine access change, and it is the one worth reading carefully. A visa-on-arrival counter closing for works, or a new electronic visa portal launching with a seventy-two-hour queue, changes what a traveller actually experiences. Here the two indexes can legitimately differ in the short term, because one reflects the legal access and the other waits for the first approvals to clear.

That difference is the whole story of the gap between a legal right and a practical one. A destination can be counted as accessible on paper and be practically unusable for weeks.

## 3. The four questions to ask before you quote a rank

Ask what the access categories are, and whether the index lumps them or splits them.
Ask for the data vintage, because indexes stamp it small at the foot of the table.
Ask whether the index measures access or ease, because a thirty-day stay on arrival with a fifty-dollar fee and a two-hour line is not the same thing as visa-free.
Ask who is publishing it and what they sell, because the same list drives an agent's pitch and a travel newsletter.

## 4. What to actually do when your passport "drops"

Open both indexes side by side. Check the refresh dates. Then call the airline desk, because the desk has to answer the question the graphic is pretending to answer, which is whether you can board and clear immigration on the day you want to travel. The airline's answer comes from the rules. The graphic comes from a count.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: My passport dropped three places. Do I have less travel access?**
A: Not necessarily. Check both indexes and their refresh dates first. If one bundles an electronic authorisation and the other splits it out, the rank moved and your access did not.

**Q: How do I tell a counting change from a real change?**
A: Look for an archived earlier edition of the same index. If the previous edition already counted the same destinations and the new one does not, the rules changed. If the two indexes simply disagree, the counting changed.

**Q: Who should I trust if the graphic and the desk disagree?**
A: The desk. Airlines and border officials work from the rules and the treaty text, not from a ranking chart.
