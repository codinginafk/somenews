**Short answer:** The claim is not supportable from any published statistic, and Sweden's own crime statistics office says why in unusually blunt terms. Brå, the Swedish National Council for Crime Prevention, states that "sometimes, simplistic or inaccurate information can be read about crime in Sweden" and that misinterpretation is "especially true when it comes to making international comparisons". The reason is mechanical. Sweden records every reported offence as a crime, counts every offence committed on a single occasion separately, counts several offences of the same kind against one victim separately, and counts attempted offences together with completed crimes. A ranking built on that basis cannot be compared with a country that counts differently.

## 1. The trap in one paragraph

Brå's own guidance on Swedish crime statistics sets out the three factors that make international comparison difficult: the legal factors, which include how offences are defined in legislation and the rules that govern police and prosecutors; the statistical factors, which include the principles for when a crime is recorded; and the extent to which crime is reported and registered at all.

Each of those varies by country. So does willingness to report, and so does the effort police put into dealing with reported crime given the way they prioritise different offences. Brå's conclusion is that these differences make international comparisons difficult, and the page exists to explain the Swedish rules so that readers can see why.

A viral clip that puts Sweden at the top of a European crime table has, at minimum, failed to apply this.

## 2. The three counting rules that decide the number

The rules are specific enough to change a table on their own.

First, reported events. Brå says Swedish statistics record all reported events as crimes even if some are later found not to have constituted criminal offences. In some countries, an event is only recorded if, after investigation, it can legitimately be considered a crime or there is sufficient evidence that one was committed.

Second, one occasion, several offences. In some countries, if several offences are committed on the same occasion, only the most serious is recorded. In Sweden, every offence committed on a single occasion is recorded in principle. The same applies to several offences of the same kind against a single victim, which some countries count as one crime and Sweden counts separately.

Third, attempts. In Swedish statistics, attempted offences are in most cases counted together with completed crimes. In a number of other countries, attempted offences are either recorded separately or ignored for statistical purposes.

Each rule pushes in the same direction for a country with high reporting and high recorded volume. A headline built by comparing Swedish police logs with a country's survey data is not comparing crime. It is comparing three counting systems.

## 3. What the survey instrument is for

Brå publishes a second, separate instrument for a different question: the Swedish Crime Survey, a survey of self-reported victimisation.

Brå describes it as a valuable indicator of exposure to crime, as a means of describing perceptions of safety or fear of crime, and as a national reference point for other surveys. That is the instrument to reach for when the question is what happened to people, and it is the one whose findings move more slowly than the administrative record.

Brå's statistics section also carries the dedicated pages for specific crime categories, including shootings and violence, where the counting notes for that category are set out. That is the practice to copy: go to the category page, read the counting rules, and only then read someone else's summary of the number.

## 4. The verdict: missing context

The claim fails. No published Swedish statistic supports "Sweden is the crime capital of Europe", and the country's own statistics office warns specifically against simplistic readings of Swedish crime data and specifically against international comparisons built without adjusting for counting rules.

What the record does support is narrower and more interesting. Sweden counts more per reported event than many countries do, and it counts attempts with completed crimes. When you read a Swedish crime number, you are reading a counting rule as much as a level of crime, and the counting rules are published, which is more than most countries offer.

That is also why the two instruments disagree, and why the survey is the one to use for "how safe do people feel" and the police log for "what was reported and recorded". ## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Is Swedish crime rising?**
A: The question cannot be answered from a cross-country chart, because the counting rules differ. Brå's own page lists three factors that make international comparison difficult and explains the Swedish rules in detail.

**Q: Why do Swedish crime numbers look higher than neighbouring countries?**
A: Partly because Sweden records every reported offence as a crime, counts every offence committed on one occasion separately, and counts attempts together with completed crimes. Those are counting choices, published by Brå.

**Q: Which number should I use for "is Sweden safe"?**
A: The Swedish Crime Survey for exposure to crime and for perceived safety, and the official statistics for what was reported and recorded. Brå presents them as complementary, not interchangeable.
