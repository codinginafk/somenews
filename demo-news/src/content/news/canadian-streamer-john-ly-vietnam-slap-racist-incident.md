**Short answer:** The clip is real, and it shows more than a slap. Canadian Kick streamer John Ly, known online as JohnWhatsGoingOn, was livestreaming inside a 7-Eleven in Saigon when he turned his camera on two Indian men. One of them asked him repeatedly not to film him. Ly kept the camera on them, used expletives, and told one of them "This is not New Delhi." The men told him he was "monetising hate", asked him to leave, and moved him towards the exit while he kept filming. A third man then slapped him, and Ly kept the broadcast running. Two claims that circulate with the video, that he had been filming them for about 20 minutes and that he made racial remarks, could not be independently verified from the footage, The Times of India reported.

## 1. What the footage shows

The Hindustan Times describes the sequence from the video that Ly himself shared on YouTube. He was livestreaming inside the convenience store when he turned the camera on two Indian men. His first question was "Which part of India are you guys from? Delhi?" They told him they were from Bengaluru.

One of the men then asked Ly not to film him and to keep the camera away. The Times of India reports the same thing in the same terms: the man repeatedly asked not to be recorded and told him to "keep the camera away". Ly continued recording and engaging with them, and the exchange became increasingly heated.

At one point, according to both reports, Ly appears to tell one of the men "This is not New Delhi." India Today records what followed. The man said "You know where I am from. Keep the camera away", and questioned Ly about privacy laws. He added: "You've been here all day, making content."

The second man then intervened. India Today records him telling Ly "You are f***ing racist" and pushing him. As the men asked Ly to leave the store, he replied "I know where you work. I know your restaurant."

One of the men then said the words that frame the clip. According to the Hindustan Times, the Times of India and Times Now, he told Ly that he was "monetising hate" and asked him to leave. The men moved him towards the exit while repeating the request to keep the camera away.

The physical moment came next, and the reports are consistent about who did it. The Hindustan Times says another man approached the streamer and slapped him while the livestream was still running. Times Now and India Today use the same wording.

What happened next is the detail that separates this from a clip of a man being hit. Ly did not end the broadcast. The Hindustan Times reports that he continued filming as the argument carried on, and that he later appears to say "No one likes Indians out here" before leaving the store. India Today reports that he continued livestreaming instead of ending the stream.

## 2. What is verified and what is not

This matters, because two specific claims travel with the video and neither survives the footage.

The Times of India reports that several posts shared on October 4 claimed Ly had been filming or bothering the men for around 20 minutes before the confrontation, and that some posts accused him of making racial remarks. It states plainly that these claims could not be independently verified from the available footage. Times Now reports the same thing with the same conclusion.

So the record supports this: two men were filmed after one asked the streamer to stop, the exchange escalated, the streamer kept the camera running, and a third man slapped him. The record does not support a 20-minute harassment timeline or a finding that the streamer made racial remarks.

There is a further limit. The Hindustan Times, which gives the most detailed account, carries the standard note on user-generated content: the report is based on social media material, it has not independently verified the claims, and it does not endorse them. Times Now describes the same footage as the source and reports that it has drawn millions of views. No official account of the incident, and no police record, appears in any of the reporting reviewed here.

## 3. The reaction was genuinely split

The responses in the reporting do not fall on one side.

The Hindustan Times records viewers who criticised Ly for continuing to film after the men repeatedly asked him to stop, and who said people should be able to ask not to be featured in livestream content. It also records viewers who said that even if the streamer was being provocative, slapping him was an unnecessary escalation. Times Now reports the same split.

One quoted response captures the reason the clip kept circulating. A viewer wrote: "He himself is a Filipino/Asian and being racist to an Indian, not really cool. And I don't support any kind of violence or physical altercation."

## 4. What the platform's own rules say

The platform's community guidelines are the rulebook that actually applied to the stream, and they are more nuanced than either side of the argument online.

Kick's guidelines set out its position on hate speech in Guideline 5. The platform says it does not permit hate speech against individuals or groups based on race, religion, sexual orientation, gender, disability or other demographic identifiers. It also states that context is crucial in evaluating reports, that words acceptable in one situation can take on a different meaning in another, and that moderation assesses the creator's intent, their level of involvement and whether content crosses into coordinated harassment or calls for action.

That last clause is the one that matters here. Whether Ly's behaviour breaches the guidelines is a moderation judgement that Kick has not published. The clip does not record what the platform decided, and no report reviewed here covers the outcome.

The privacy question raised by one of the men in the store, about whether filming strangers is lawful in Vietnam, is a real one, and the Electronic Frontier Foundation's work on digital privacy is the standing reference on it. What none of these sources establish is what the law in that specific store on that specific day permits. The argument in the clip is about consent and respect, not about a statute.

## 5. The verdict: missing context

The claim is not false. Ly was slapped, and the slap was unnecessary by the standards the reporting quotes from viewers themselves. Physical assault is physical assault.

It is also incomplete in the way that matters for understanding the clip. Ly was filming two men who had asked him to stop. The assault came after a man told him he was "monetising hate" and after the men moved him towards the exit. The slapper was a third man. And the streamer kept broadcasting through all of it, which is the part that turns a street argument into a published one.

The same framing problem shows up wherever a clip is treated as the whole event, which is why we read [data and narratives separately when a crime story breaks](/britain-crime-claim-vs-data/), and why [safety maps get read for their method before their colour](/safest-country-maps-footnote/).

A video can show an assault and still be edited by what happens on either side of it. This one was not edited. It was just watched in pieces.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Was the streamer provoked before he was slapped?**
A: The footage shows a heated exchange, and one of the men told him he was "monetising hate" before asking him to leave. The reports do not settle whether the encounter before the store was provocation, which is why the claim of 20 minutes of filming is unverified.

**Q: Did the streamer make racist remarks?**
A: The reporting does not establish it. One of the men told him "You are f***ing racist", and The Times of India reports that social media accusations of the streamer making racial remarks could not be independently verified from the footage.

**Q: Is it legal to film people in public in Vietnam?**
A: One of the men raised privacy laws on camera, but none of the sources reviewed here establishes what Vietnamese law permits in that situation. What is documented is that the platform's own guidelines applied to the broadcast.

**Q: Was the slap justified?**
A: The reporting shows viewers divided, with many calling the escalation unnecessary even while criticising the streamer. No official or legal outcome is reported.
