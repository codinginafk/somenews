**Short answer:** While the viral video shows Canadian streamer John Ly being physically slapped by an Indian tourist in a Saigon 7-Eleven, the footage circulating online strips away critical context: the streamer was engaging in aggressive "rage-baiting," ignoring repeated requests to stop filming, and hurling racial slurs.

## 1. What the viral post shows

The viral incident involving the slapping of Canadian Kick streamer John Ly, known online as *JohnWhatsGoingOn*, has sparked massive debate across social media platforms. Much of the online response has heavily focused on the physical assault aspect of the video. Commenters have rushed to point fingers at the Indian tourists, framing them as the aggressors in a foreign country (often leaning on biased assumptions rather than actual events, a trend we have analyzed before regarding [how data and narratives can be manipulated](/britain-crime-claim-vs-data/)).

## 2. What the full footage actually says

When reviewing the unedited broadcast, the situation changes entirely. The Indian tourists were visibly uncomfortable being filmed and made multiple polite requests for Ly to stop recording them. Rather than respecting their privacy, the streamer leaned into the harassment, hurling racial slurs and escalating the situation.

This is a textbook example of "rage-baiting." Streamers intentionally take advantage of the liberal laws surrounding public filming in Southeast Asia (a region often perceived as highly welcoming to digital nomads, as noted in our coverage of [safe country maps](/safest-country-maps-footnote/)) to deliberately antagonize foreigners and minorities. The goal is simple: provoke a reaction to generate viral content and boost viewership.

In this specific case, before the physical altercation occurred, one of the two men involved directly called out the streamer's intentions, asking, "You're filming for hate? You are monetizing hate!"

## 3. What's missing from the conversation

The viral framing completely omits the issue of digital consent. While public filming may be legally permissible in many jurisdictions, using a platform to objectify, racially abuse, and harass individuals crosses a clear ethical line. The incident highlights an ongoing trend of casual racism directed at Indians living or traveling abroad, which is often rewarded by streaming platform algorithms that favor high-engagement, controversial content. 

Have we lost ourselves to the point where getting satisfied watching people being objectified and violated is acceptable for digital entertainment? The conversation needs to shift from the physical slap to the systemic issue of platforms turning a blind eye to monetized harassment.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Is it legal to film people in public in Vietnam?**
A: Generally, filming in public spaces is less restricted in many Southeast Asian countries compared to strict privacy laws in the West. However, using that footage to harass or racially abuse individuals violates the terms of service of almost all major streaming platforms.

**Q: What is rage-baiting?**
A: Rage-baiting is the practice of intentionally doing or saying offensive, controversial, or annoying things specifically to provoke an angry reaction from the target or the audience, thereby driving up engagement and views.
