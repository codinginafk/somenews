**Short answer:** It was not a paper mix-up, and it was not an isolated incident. Six pallets of Nvidia hardware worth a reported $11.4 million left a depot in Oakland for a data centre build in Fort Worth, Texas, and never arrived. The Oakland Police Department is treating it as a grand theft, filed the report on September 13, 2026, and has announced no arrests and no recovery. The wider pattern is the story. Ars Technica reports thieves have taken more than $100 million in similar shipments since June, and FreightWaves points to nearly $111 million in stolen data centre freight.

*Correction (2026-11-15): This article was published on November 8, 2026 citing publisher homepages rather than the underlying reports. It has been rebuilt on six verified articles. Two claims have been corrected or withdrawn. The August 29 date is the pickup date, not a confirmed theft date, and police say the September 13 complaint date does not establish when the theft happened. The earlier version also said thieves had inside knowledge of the cargo's contents. Police say they have not established whether the food-hauler truck was legitimate or impersonated. Unsourced figures on GPU chassis prices, delivery delays, insurance terms and serial-number stripping have been removed.*

## 1. What actually happened

Six pallets of Nvidia computer hardware worth a reported $11.4 million disappeared during a shipment from California to Texas, FreightWaves reports.
A truck collected the merchandise in Oakland on August 29, 2026, for delivery to Fort Worth.
The vehicle displayed a food transportation company's logo during the scheduled pickup, according to The Mercury News, the Bay Area News Group outlet that broke the case.
The driver then travelled south, making several stops lasting multiple hours. Police attempting to trace the route found the truck had made those extended stops before losing it somewhere in San Bernardino County.
The theft was not noticed until early September, when the buyers realised the shipment had never been received.
The Oakland Police Department confirmed an ongoing grand theft investigation. In a statement to FreightWaves, the department said it is investigating a grand theft that occurred in East Oakland, that the victim reported the crime on September 13, 2026, and that anyone with information should contact its General Crimes Unit.
Nvidia did not respond to FreightWaves' request for comment, and Inbound Logistics reports the company has not commented publicly.

Two facts in that sequence do more work than the headline number. The first is the gap between pickup and complaint. Inbound Logistics notes the report was made 15 days after pickup. The second is what police do not know. According to FreightWaves, they have not confirmed the reported $11.4 million valuation or identified the specific merchandise, and they have not identified the cargo's owner, shipper, motor carrier or driver.

## 2. The pattern: more than $100 million since June

Ars Technica, citing its own reporter Ashley Belanger, says thieves have made off with more than $100 million in similar shipments since June. FreightWaves, with the same roundup in view, describes nearly $111 million in stolen data centre freight involving other high-value technology shipments.

Older incidents form the background. TechSpot reports that in 2025, investigations in Fremont and Union City involved more than $7.5 million in losses for Nvidia, and that in June 2026 authorities recovered $1.3 million of equipment in Chicago after GPS trackers helped police find the stolen goods. Ars Technica notes, on the Mercury News reporting, that police believe the 2025 burglaries were carried out by a group that obtained vehicles for the heist in Southern California before travelling north to the Bay Area.

The fraud method also has a name. Inbound Logistics, citing Verisk CargoNet, says fictional pickups, where criminals pose as a legitimate carrier to collect a load, made up about a quarter of reported cargo theft incidents in the third quarter of 2026. The same source says Verisk CargoNet recorded 19 thefts worth $1 million or more in that quarter, many involving electronics and data centre materials.

TechSpot describes the digital entry point: thieves use hacking and phishing to impersonate legitimate shipping companies, then bid on the load and drive away with it.

## 3. Why the economics work

The reason the crime exists is a price gap, and it is documented.

Olaf Groth, a professor at UC Berkeley's Haas School of Business, told ABC News, as reported by Ars Technica, that if a chip costs about $550,000, then they go for about $1 million or more abroad. Wccftech quotes him further, saying these chips command about double the price they command inside the United States and calling them a national security asset. He said there are lots and lots of incentives to hijack those chips anywhere where security is weak.

Ars Technica describes the same logic in one line: stolen components can be stripped down and sold in gray-market transactions or shipped overseas where access to US tech hardware may be restricted.

Two smaller episodes show how thieves misread a target. TechSpot reports that in September, thieves stole Nvidia-branded trailers expecting GPUs and instead found 20,000 pounds of sand, because PlusAI, the company whose logo was on the trucks, used sand to simulate loads for research and development. FreightWaves adds that the two trailers were taken from a Fremont warehouse, that they held approximately 40,000 pounds of sand between them, and that police recovered both in nearby Newark. Neither outlet found a link between that theft and the Oakland case.

The other miss was in Brazil. Wccftech reported that an RTX 5090 shipment worth almost $500,000 was stolen from a Brazilian airport in the month before the Oakland theft.

## 4. The countermeasures, and what they cost

Ars Technica, citing investigators quoted by NBC News, says the criminals have shown planning and sophistication by using GPS location spoofing and even arranging collisions with police escort vehicles. That is the detail that changes the threat model. A hijack used to mean a knife at a rest stop.

Shippers are responding with visibility and monitoring. TechSpot reports they are investing more in monitoring services and deploying Flock cameras at gates to watch for suspicious people and license plates. The same report says any truck displaying Nvidia's logo might be targeted, regardless of whether it carries anything valuable.

Inbound Logistics frames the underlying problem in one line: a single shipment can be worth millions of dollars and often moves as ordinary dry freight. The freight itself is unremarkable. The value inside it is not.

## 5. What the record does not settle

This is the part the viral posts skip, and it is a long list.

Police have not said which Nvidia products were on the six pallets, and they have not confirmed the reported $11.4 million valuation. They have not established whether the food-hauler truck was legitimate or impersonated, and they have not publicly explained the extended stops. They have not announced arrests or confirmed any recovery of the merchandise.

Ars Technica puts it plainly: authorities have not said what Nvidia hardware was stolen or exactly how the theft was carried out. FreightWaves adds that authorities have not linked the Oakland investigation to a separate $12 million cargo theft case involving suspects who allegedly exploited established trucking identities to obtain freight.

What is documented is the shape of the crime, the scale of the wave, and the reason it works. The rest is still an open case.

## 6. The verdict: false, with context

The claim fails. A clerical error does not explain a truck that drove past its destination, vanished in San Bernardino County after hours-long stops, and went unreported for two weeks while the cargo moved on. The case is a grand theft investigation with no arrests.

The context matters for a different reason. One shipment is not a pattern. FreightWaves, Ars Technica, TechSpot and Verisk CargoNet all describe a wave running at a scale where a single thief's gain is measured in the hundreds of thousands of dollars and the loss is measured in the tens of millions. The September PlusAI trailer theft is the clearest illustration of what that does to criminals' incentives: they now break into yards for what is on the badge.

That is the same pattern we traced in [the $6.3 billion crypto theft](/binance-covers-90-percent-losses-fbi-north-korea-hack/) and in the [2026 wave of institutional data leaks](/top-5-data-leaks-2026-php2shell-state-bank/): a valuable asset with weak physical or administrative control invites organised actors, and the market for the goods does the rest.

A: Because of the resale gap. Olaf Groth of UC Berkeley's Haas School of Business told ABC News that if a chip costs about $550,000, then they go for about $1 million or more abroad, roughly double the price they command inside the United States.

**Q: How do criminals know which trucks to target?**
