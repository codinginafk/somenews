**Short answer:** Finland's position at the top of the World Happiness Report is a real result, and it is also a national average built from six inputs, two of which are income measures and one of which is perceptions of corruption. It says nothing directly about how a February evening feels. Safety is measured by a different instrument entirely, Gallup's Law and Order Index, which asks four questions about feeling safe walking at night, trust in local police, theft and assault. Reading the two together gives a much better picture of Helsinki than either ranking does on its own.

## 1. What the happiness score is made of

The World Happiness Report publishes its method and its country table for every edition, and the current edition is the 2026 report. The ranking is assembled from six inputs: GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy at birth, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption.

Three of those six are survey-derived and three are structural or economic. Two, income per head and perceived corruption, are the ones that move national scores the most.

That structure is why the score reads the way it does for Finland. A high income per head, high social support and a corruption perception that is near the top of the table all push in the same direction, and they are measured over a population rather than over a day. A resident's assessment of their own life on a given Tuesday is not one of the columns.

For the wider explanation of what this ranking does and does not capture, see our [piece on Finland and Bangladesh](/finland-happiest-bangladesh-reality/).

## 2. How safety is measured, separately

Public safety has its own instrument, and it is worth reading the questions in full because they are easy to misdescribe.

Gallup's Law and Order Index is collected through the Gallup World Poll and uses four questions to gauge people's sense of personal security. The positive responses to those four questions produce the score, and a higher score means a higher proportion of the population reporting that they feel safe.

The questions are these. Do you feel safe walking alone at night in the city or area where you live? Do you have confidence in the local police force in the city or area where you live? Within the last 12 months, have you had money or property stolen from you or another household member? Within the past 12 months, have you been assaulted or mugged?

What makes the index usable is its sampling. Gallup says the results are based on nationally representative, probability-based samples among the adult population aged 15 and older in 144 countries and territories in 2024, gathered by telephone or face-to-face survey with approximately 1,000 or more respondents in each country or territory. The margin of sampling error on the total national adult sample ranges between plus or minus 3.3 and plus or minus 5.4 percentage points at 95% confidence.

The latest reported world score is 83 out of 100. Finland is not the only high scorer, and the list is not a Nordic monopoly: Tajikistan scores 97, Singapore 95, Kosovo 94, and China, Iceland and Vietnam each 93. In Europe the leaders are Kosovo at 94, Iceland at 93, Norway at 92, Austria and Switzerland at 91, and Denmark and Finland both at 90.

## 3. What a city score cannot carry

A national index cannot carry three things that matter most to someone reading about a city.

The first is weather. Finland's winters are long and dark, and seasonal light has a documented effect on mood. A survey administered in February and one administered in June do not measure the same country, and the World Happiness Report does not publish seasonal breakdowns.

The second is housing cost. The happiness score has no rent column. A city can score at the top of a national table and still be the hardest place in that country for a newcomer to afford a flat, because the index has no input for it.

The third is the labour market for newcomers. Trust in institutions and a high national score say nothing about how long a job callback takes for a recent arrival, and that is a question about the city's labour market, not about its index position.

## 4. The verdict: missing context

The claim fails. Finland topping the happiness ranking is not evidence that Helsinki residents feel safe and secure all year round, because the ranking does not measure safety at all, and because it is a national annual average rather than a description of a city in a particular season.

What survives the claim is narrower and still worth saying. Finland does score at the top of the World Happiness Report on the six inputs it uses, and it scores 90 out of 100 on Gallup's Law and Order Index, which does measure safety and does so with a national probability sample of about 1,000 or more respondents per country.

Two independent instruments, two different questions, one country at the top of both. That is a stronger finding than the viral version, because it is one you can check.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Is Finland the safest country in the world?**
A: On Gallup's Law and Order Index, Finland scores 90 out of 100, and nine other countries score 91 or higher, including Tajikistan at 97, Singapore at 95, Bahrain, Oman and Saudi Arabia at 91. Top of one ranking is not the same as top of every ranking.

**Q: Does a high happiness score mean people are happy every day?**
A: No. It is an annual national average over six inputs, none of which is about a given season, a given street or a given rent cheque.

**Q: Which index should I use for safety questions?**
A: Gallup's Law and Order Index, because it asks four safety-specific questions and publishes its sampling frame and margin of error alongside the score.
