Finland tops the ladder most years. Helsinki in February shows why, and what the number skips.

I landed on a Tuesday. Minus 9. Light from 9 to 3. The tram ran on time to the minute. A nurse named Aino told me she leaves her bike unlocked outside the clinic. "It will be there," she said. She has done it for six years. That habit is the score. Trust, repeated daily.

Three things push the average up:

- Pay arrives. Sick leave works. Child care costs stay capped. People answer "7" or "8" because bills do not spike.
- Streets feel calm. I walked Kallio at midnight. Quiet. One bar queue, no push.
- Paperwork is short. Tax, clinic booking, library card. Each took minutes. Low friction reads as high trust.

Two things locals raised within an hour:

Darkness drags mood. A student named Oskari said November hits him each year. "Score high, feel low," he joked. His joke points at method. The ladder asks about life overall, not today.

Rents bite. A one-bed near the center runs 900 to 1,200 euro. Newcomers share flats or move out. Integration splits views. Some Finns cite smooth school intake. Some Somali and Iraqi parents I met cite slow job callbacks despite fluent Finnish. Same city, two job hunts.

So read it straight: high trust plus solid floors lift the mean to near 7.7. Weather, housing, and hiring gaps shape days without moving the mean much. A rank can be right on method and thin on life. This brief keeps both.
