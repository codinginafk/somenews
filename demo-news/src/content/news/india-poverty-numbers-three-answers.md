**Short answer:** Three published figures describe poverty in India: **2.6 percent**, **14.96 percent** and **29.5 percent**. None is a typo. They count different things. The World Bank uses an international extreme-poverty line of $2.15 a day, NITI Aayog counts deprivation across twelve indicators, and the Rangarajan panel used a national consumption basket. A post that quotes one of these as "India's poverty rate" without naming the line has changed the question.

## 1. The viral version

The claim that travels is short: only 2.6 percent of Indians are poor. It is usually shared as a screenshot of a World Bank chart, with no date, no unit footnote and no mention of how the line was set. The number itself is real. We pulled the World Bank series on November 19, 2026 and found **2.6** for the latest available year, **2023**, on the `SI.POV.DDAY` indicator.

That indicator measures the headcount below **$2.15 a day in 2017 purchasing power parity**. It is an international threshold designed to compare extreme poverty across countries. It is not a measure of whether a family in India can afford rent, school fees or a medical bill.

## 2. The second answer: 14.96 percent

NITI Aayog's National Multidimensional Poverty Index uses a different question entirely. Instead of income or consumption, it checks twelve deprivations: nutrition, health, education, housing, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, assets and others, drawn from the National Family Health Survey.

The 2023 progress review reports that the share of multidimensionally poor people fell from **24.85 percent in 2015-16** to **14.96 percent in 2019-21**. The Press Information Bureau adds that **13.5 crore** people left multidimensional poverty over that period.

This figure can be higher than the World Bank number because it catches people who are not destitute by a global standard but still lack several basic services. It can also be lower than a consumption-based national line because it does not count a household as poor purely on spending.

## 3. The third answer: 29.5 percent

The Rangarajan expert group, whose report was summarised by the Press Information Bureau in 2014, reverted to separate all-India rural and urban poverty baskets after the Tendulkar panel had used a single reference basket. A working paper from the Institute for studies in Industrial Development records the group's estimate of **29.5 percent** below the poverty line in **2011-12**, with an all-India line of about **₹1,105 per person per month**.

That line is a national consumption threshold, built from what a household needs to meet basic norms in India. It sits at a different point from $2.15 a day and it uses a different year, so it cannot be compared with either of the other two as a trend.

## 4. What the three numbers are actually measuring

| Figure | Source | Year | What it counts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.6% | World Bank | 2023 | Below $2.15/day, 2017 PPP |
| 14.96% | NITI Aayog | 2019-21 | Deprived on 12 indicators |
| 29.5% | Rangarajan | 2011-12 | Below a national consumption line |

Three different lines, three different years and three different units. Putting them in one sentence as competing answers to the same question is the error. They are answers to three questions.

This is the same mistake we describe in [our guide to reading rankings](/how-to-read-rankings/): a number without its recipe is not a finding. And it connects directly to [our GDP comparison](/india-gdp-1991-vs-now-data/), where the headline total says nothing about distribution.

## 5. The verdict

The claim that only 2.6 percent of Indians are poor is **missing context**, not false. The figure is published by the World Bank for 2023. What the viral version leaves out is that the line is an international extreme-poverty threshold, that the year is 2023, and that two other official measures put the share several times higher using different definitions.

A fair sentence would name all three and explain the lines. A fair post would also say which question it is trying to answer.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Which poverty figure is correct?**
A: All three are correctly computed against their own definitions. They answer different questions, so there is no single number without first choosing a line.

**Q: Why is the World Bank figure so much lower?**
A: It uses an international extreme-poverty threshold of $2.15 a day in 2017 PPP. That is a lower bar than a national consumption basket or a twelve-indicator deprivation measure.

**Q: Did poverty actually fall in India?**
A: On the NITI multidimensional measure, yes: 24.85 percent in 2015-16 to 14.96 percent in 2019-21. That is a different series from the World Bank figure and uses data from NFHS.
