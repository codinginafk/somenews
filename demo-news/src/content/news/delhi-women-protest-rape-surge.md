**Short answer:** The protests are real and they are large, and they are aimed at something more precise than a general cry about safety. After a 17-year-old girl was allegedly gang-raped in Aastha Kunj Park in South Delhi by three men posing as police officers, hundreds of Delhi University students marched in rain, at the Faculty of Arts and near Lady Shri Ram, Gargi and Kamala Nehru colleges, in what organisers called the third and largest demonstration in a week. Their grievance is not that nothing happened. It is that the law changed after 2012 and the enforcement never caught up.

## 1. What happened and when

The Indian Express, reporting from the protest, sets out the sequence. A 17-year-old girl was allegedly gang-raped at Aastha Kunj Park in South Delhi. In the days that followed, marches took place near Lady Shri Ram College, Gargi College and Kamala Nehru College. On Monday evening, September 28, hundreds of students gathered at the DU Faculty of Arts for what organisers said was the third and largest such demonstration in a week.

The Indian Express describes the scene precisely, and the detail matters because it is what distinguishes this from a social media trend. "Rain, loud sloganeering, placards with messages for authorities, and drenched students trying to 'force a change'." The protest was open, organisers said, "strictly for women and queer participants."

Khaleej Times, reporting the same case and the wider wave, adds what the authorities said. In that incident, three men posing as police officers allegedly gang-raped the 17-year-old in a public park in the heart of the capital. The paper also notes a separate case in which a man offered a student a lift during heavy rain, and a video that surfaced in the month showing a teenage girl being sexually assaulted in the eastern state of Bihar. Each case has brought arrests, political condemnation and public outcry.

On September 30, the Students' Federation of India led a march to the Delhi Secretariat over rising sexual violence against women and targeted the BJP, The New Indian Express reported. The Guardian's coverage from the same day was headlined on the same theme: "We want justice".

## 2. What the students said

The Indian Express recorded a second-year student at Shri Ram College of Commerce, Varsha, who kept track of the time during the demonstration. Her account is about what daily life costs when the state is not trusted. "My father tracks my location continuously. He keeps a check … does not want me to be out once the darkness falls. It's deeply frustrating… Had I been a son, such severe restrictions would never have existed."

The paper also records protesters on Monday giving examples from Punjab and Bihar, and arguing that women were not better off even in the Capital, as they recounted the challenges they face on the commute and the restrictions they face from their own families.

That second element is the one that is easy to miss. Part of what the march was about is the cost of the protection that replaces police: location tracking, curfews, and the assumption that a daughter needs to be indoors by dark while a son does not.

## 3. Why 2012 keeps coming back

The Khaleej Times makes the connection explicit. The case evokes the 2012 gang rape and murder of a 23-year-old physical therapy student on a bus, which triggered nationwide protests and led to more robust laws against sexual violence.

What changed after 2012, the same report sets out, is substantial. The government expanded the criminal definitions of penetration and consent. Gang rape became punishable by at least 20 years in prison. The death sentence was introduced for repeat rape offenders.

The Guardian's reporting from September 30 carries the same point from the other side: women in the protests say nothing has changed since the 2012 case, and campaigners say the changes have proven inadequate.

And here is the sentence that explains the mood better than any statistic. The problem is not in the law but with the people who are implementing it, said criminal lawyer Shilpi Jain, who has represented survivors of sexual violence. Overstretched police forces and courts lack the means to investigate cases thoroughly enough, which hinders the reforms' impact. A women's rights activist in the same reporting put it more bluntly: "Nobody is working on the actual solution, which is the mindset change."

## 4. What the record does not settle

Two things are open, and one of them is material to any claim that police did nothing.

The cases themselves are described in the reporting as allegations, with arrests made. What the sources reviewed here establish is the protest and the state response, not the outcome of the prosecution. Anyone telling you the courts have already delivered a verdict is going beyond the record.

The second gap is the enforcement claim itself. The New Indian Express, on September 28, examined Delhi's women's safety net and found it full of gaps despite audits, budgets and emergency systems, reporting that a series of alleged sexual assault, stalking and harassment cases involving women and minor girls across Delhi that September renewed scrutiny of the capital's public-safety system. That is an audit finding, not a verdict on any individual case, and the distinction is the same one the protesters are making: they are not saying nothing was done, they are saying what was done was not enough.

## 5. The verdict: true, with the context that names the target

The claim is true in its plainest reading. Women and students were protesting in large numbers in Delhi in September 2026, the immediate cause was a string of cases of alleged sexual violence including the alleged gang rape of a 17-year-old in Aastha Kunj Park, and the recurring theme of the coverage is institutional inaction.

The context that the claim leaves out is the target. These were not general safety demonstrations and they were not primarily about one case. The sustained theme across the reporting is the gap between a 14-year-old legal settlement and the enforcement of it, and the private cost that gap transfers onto the families of the women who live under it.

That is a harder thing to protest against than a single failure, and it is why the demonstrations ran for a week and grew each time. The Indian Express recorded the scale in one line: the third and largest demonstration in a week, in the rain, with placards addressed to the authorities.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: What triggered the Delhi protests?**
A: A 17-year-old girl was allegedly gang-raped at Aastha Kunj Park in South Delhi by three men posing as police officers, after which marches were held near Lady Shri Ram, Gargi and Kamala Nehru colleges and then at the DU Faculty of Arts.

**Q: Was this a one-off reaction to one case?**
A: No. The Indian Express reported the Faculty of Arts gathering as the third and largest demonstration in a week, and the coverage records marches across multiple cities.

**Q: What are the demonstrators demanding?**
A: Better safety measures for women and, on the reporting's own framing, enforcement of the legal changes made after the 2012 case. The legal response since 2012 has been expanded and strengthened, and campaigners say it has not been implemented.

**Q: Has the prosecution concluded?**
A: No. The sources reviewed here describe the cases as allegations with arrests made. The protest and the enforcement record are documented; the trial outcome is not.
