It did not record that the IEA still assessed the market as being in deficit, or that physical Brent prices have stayed far above the futures curve. The verdict has been changed from "False, with context" to "Misleading, missing context", and every source now points to the specific report or article.*

**Short answer:** The claim guarantees something the data does not support. Two of the most credible forecasters in the world disagree about September 2026 by nearly 1.9 million barrels a day, and both can be right. JPMorgan puts the market back in surplus, at 1.4 million barrels per day in October. The International Energy Agency's October report still shows a deficit and forecasts a fourth-quarter deficit of about 1.8 million barrels per day. The physical market says barrels are ample right now while the forward market says they will be scarce in 2027, and the physical price of oil says something different from both.

## 1. What the shock actually was

The market was widely expected to be in surplus at the start of 2026. The outbreak of the Iran war in late February overturned that, in what the International Energy Agency called the world's biggest ever supply disruption.

The IEA's own March 11 press release, reported by NACS, recorded that the conflict "impeded oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz, with export volumes of crude and refined products currently at less than 10% of pre-conflict levels". For scale, an average of 20 million barrels per day of crude and products transited the strait in 2025, around a quarter of the world's seaborne oil trade.

On the same day the IEA's 32 member countries voted unanimously to release 400 million barrels from emergency reserves. The Guardian reported that this was about a third of the group's total government stockpiles and more than double the previous largest action, the 182 million barrels released in 2022 after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The UK's contribution was 13.5 million barrels, Japan's about 80 million, South Korea's 22.46 million and Germany's 19.51 million. Macquarie analysts put the scale of it at about four days of global production.

Prices responded hard. Brent briefly jumped as high as $119.50 a barrel on the Monday before the decision, a level not seen since 2022, and then rose nearly 4% to just over $91 on the day itself, The Guardian reported.

## 2. The case for a surplus

The Reuters wire report carried by ETEnergyworld, Yahoo Finance and the Gulf Times lays out the JPMorgan argument in full. Recovering Middle Eastern flows, rising production outside the Gulf and falling demand pushed global supply above consumption again. JPMorgan's estimate, quoted verbatim, was that "the global oil market returned to a surplus of 1.1 million barrels per day in September, widening to 1.4 million bpd in October, nearly matching last year's average of 1.3 million bpd". Global crude imports in October rose above the average 2025 level while demand lagged.

Goldman Sachs, in the same report, estimated that global stocks did not decline in September to October after falling sizeably in the months after the war began. Goldman saw the market as broadly balanced across those two months, against a deficit in March.

The tanker data supports the recovery story with detail. Ship-tracking firm Vortexa put crude and condensate exports from the Gulf at an average 14 million barrels per day in October, about 86 percent of pre-war levels, with refined product exports at 3.1 million barrels per day, just over half of normal volumes. Middle East shipments did edge lower in October from September, which was the highest since the war started, and two ship-tracking firms said those October figures could still be revised upwards.

Oil & Gas Journal, reporting the JPMorgan work in full, adds the numbers behind the balance. Middle Eastern oil exports recovered to about 20.7 million barrels per day in October from a low of 9.8 million in March. Global crude imports reached 44.9 million barrels per day, up 1.3 million year on year. Latin American crude production rose by about 1.5 million barrels per day year on year on gains in Brazil, Venezuela, Guyana and Argentina. And global oil demand fell by an estimated 3.3 million barrels per day since the conflict began in late February.

That last figure is the mechanism behind everything else. Demand destruction did more to rebalance the market than any supply recovery.

## 3. The case against, which is the stronger one

The IEA does not agree, and the distance between the two assessments is the number worth knowing. Oil & Gas Journal reports that the IEA's October 14 Oil Market Report estimated a supply deficit of about 790,000 barrels per day in September, against JPMorgan's surplus of 1.1 million. That is a gap of nearly 1.9 million barrels per day on the same month.

The IEA also expected the tightness to persist. It forecast a fourth-quarter supply deficit of about 1.8 million barrels per day, on global supply of 101.7 million against demand of 103.5 million. It counted global observed oil inventories at 509 million barrels below end-February levels at the end of September, and reported that refined product and LPG shipments from the Middle East remained below half their pre-war levels even as crude recovered.

Two forecasts, opposite signs, same year. The reason they differ is that they cut the data at different points and treat inventory coverage differently, Oil & Gas Journal notes. Neither is wrong about the direction of travel. They disagree about how much of it has already happened.

## 4. The price curve says both things at once

The cleanest evidence in the Reuters reporting is in the shape of the Brent curve, and it is worth reading carefully because it refuses to pick a side.

London Stock Exchange Group data shows the Brent swaps curve, made up of contracts for differences, has moved into contango. The contract for delivery this week trades below the one for the following week, at the steepest discount since July, and the second- and third-week contracts also trade at a discount to the next week. Contango means oil available now is cheaper than oil promised later, which reflects a perception that prompt supply is ample.

Brent crude futures from January 2027 delivery remain in the opposite structure, known as backwardation, where prompt prices sit above later contracts. That indicates supply remains tight in the near term, or more precisely that it is expected to remain tight later on.

Adi Imsirovic, a veteran oil trader and market expert, told Reuters that Brent futures could also move into a contango structure if the swaps stay there, since changes in market structure usually start in the physical market. That is a conditional, not a prediction, and the distinction matters. A curve in contango says barrels are plentiful this week. A curve in backwardation says the market expects scarcity next year. Both statements are about the same oil.

## 5. Why prices are still high anyway

Goldman Sachs Research published its own account on October 16, 2026, and it complicates the surplus story considerably. Daan Struyven, co-head of global commodities research and head of oil research, expects Brent to trade at $90 in December 2026, a slight increase from a recent forecast of $85, and to average $80 a barrel in 2027.

Physical prices are far above that. Struyven reports that a barrel of dated Brent crude scheduled for delivery in the next month is trading at more than $130, while benchmark Brent futures have recently been above $100. He estimates spot Brent includes a nearly $25 risk premium on top of fair value, because supply could be curtailed and inventories drawn down further if there is an escalation.

His explanation is that "physical prices remain elevated because escalation threatens supply while near-record-low global inventories fuel strong restocking demand". Goldman estimates Persian Gulf oil exports have rebounded to around their 2025 average since late September, including an estimated 4 million barrels per day of "dark" exports carried by tankers running with their transponders off.

Goldman's balance-sheet view is that the market was nearly balanced in September, with demand exceeding supply by just 0.3 million barrels per day, a dramatic easing from the 7.2 million barrel per day deficit in March. Its base case is that the balance shifts to a surplus in the fourth quarter of 2026 and in 2027. On demand, Goldman puts third-quarter 2026 oil demand 3.3 million barrels per day lower year on year, with weakness concentrated in China, the Middle East and OECD Asia, and estimates it was nearly 4 million barrels per day below the path it would have taken with no oil shock. It assumes 80 percent of that gap unwinds by the fourth quarter of 2027.

None of that makes the market loose. It makes it loosening from a very tight starting point, and that is a different claim from the one the viral version makes.

## 6. The verdict

The claim has two parts and both fail as stated.

"Prolonged crude oil shortages" is contradicted by JPMorgan's surplus estimate, by Goldman seeing a broadly balanced market with a fourth-quarter surplus as the base case, by the contango in prompt Brent swaps, and by the tanker data showing Gulf crude exports back at 86 percent of pre-war levels.

But "shortages" is not a claim the evidence supports either, and that is where the viral version and the bullish version both overshoot. The IEA still forecasts a deficit. Inventories sit 509 million barrels below end-February. Dated Brent trades above $130 and carries a $25 risk premium. The 2027 futures curve is in backwardation. Goldman expects $80 Brent in 2027, which is higher than it was in 2025.

"Through 2027" is where the claim collapses hardest, because the forecast most cited for that year is an average of $80 a barrel and a return to surplus, not a shortage.

What the record supports is narrower and more useful: the physical oil market is tight now, prompt barrels are getting cheaper week to week, inventories are depleted, and the forecasters disagree by 1.9 million barrels a day about which side of balance the market is on. For anyone budgeting fuel costs or reading a central bank, that is a materially different picture from a guaranteed shortage, and a materially different one from a glut. Reading two credible forecasts as a contradiction rather than a signal about how uncertain the number is, the subject of our [guide to reading rankings](/how-to-read-rankings/), is the error to avoid.

The wider macro chain is the one we tracked through [post-election market selloffs](/wall-street-stock-market-crash-ai-bubble-midterms/) and through [pay commission and cost-of-living debates](/central-govt-da-hike-8th-pay-commission-arrears/): energy prices feed transport costs, transport costs feed headline inflation, and inflation decides what central banks do next.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Is there an oil glut or an oil shortage right now?**
A: The two most credible forecasters say opposite things about September 2026. JPMorgan puts it at a surplus of 1.1 million barrels per day and the IEA at a deficit of about 790,000 barrels per day, a gap of nearly 1.9 million barrels per day. The IEA expects a fourth-quarter deficit of about 1.8 million barrels per day.

**Q: Why are crude prices above $100 if the market has a surplus?**
A: Because inventories are depleted rather than plentiful. Goldman Sachs Research reports dated Brent for next-month delivery above $130 and estimates a nearly $25 risk premium in spot prices, while the IEA counted global observed inventories 509 million barrels below end-February levels at the end of September.

**Q: Did the war stop Gulf oil exports?**
A: It cut them to less than 10 percent of pre-conflict levels, according to the IEA in March. By October, Vortexa data put Gulf crude and condensate exports at 14 million barrels per day, about 86 percent of pre-war volumes, though refined product exports were still only a little over half of normal.

**Q: Does the contango in Brent swaps mean prices will fall through 2027?**
A: No. Contango in the prompt swaps says barrels are ample now. Brent futures from January 2027 delivery remain in backwardation, and Goldman Sachs expects Brent to average $80 a barrel in 2027 rather than to keep rising.
