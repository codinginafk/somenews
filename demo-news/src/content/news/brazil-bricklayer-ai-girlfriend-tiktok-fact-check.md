**Short answer:** The farm was not a farm. Edimar Martins, 49, a bricklayer from Imperatriz in the Brazilian state of Maranhão, sold all of his possessions on October 9, 2026 to fund a bus ticket to Belo Horizonte, 1,931 km away, after six years of following a TikTok profile called Jéssica Oliveira, also known as Jéssica Fazendeira. The profile had more than 300,000 followers. Its videos showed a woman on a farm offering men a home and full financial support. The operator of the account later published a video conceding that the character was made of artificial intelligence and that the intention was to gain engagement for sale.

## 1. What the account promised

Livemint, IBTimes and the New York Post all describe the same account. It appeared under the names Jéssica Oliveira and Jéssica Fazendeira, and drew more than 300,000 followers on TikTok. The videos showed a woman living a rural life on a farm, and the account's premise was straightforward: come and live with me, I will support you.

The New York Post quotes the invitation directly from the persona's own clip. She said: "I really want to support you, I want you to come to my farm and never work again in your life."

IBTimes describes the profile as attracting more than 300,000 followers on TikTok and featuring videos of a woman on a farm offering potential partners a home and financial support. Livemint adds that the account also ran on Facebook and Kwai, and that Martins first came across it in 2020.

The detail that explains why he believed it matters. Livemint reports that Martins had been dealing with loneliness after his mother's death. A stranger's six-year-old promise of a house and no work was not a hard sell to a man in that state.

## 2. What happened when he arrived

On Friday, October 9, according to IBTimes, Martins sold his personal belongings to pay for the bus ticket. He arrived in Belo Horizonte on Sunday, October 11.

Carrying printed photographs of the woman, he searched the city and asked passers-by and shopkeepers whether they recognised her. Nobody could. IBTimes reports he eventually sought help at Rádio Itatiaia, where he was told the woman did not exist. The New York Post quotes him directly: "I went to the radio station there, and they said no, that's fake news, that woman doesn't exist."

Livemint records what he said about the trip itself: "I sold all my things, I got rid of everything." And on why he came: "It only brings sadness, discouragement, depression. Many people wanted to find a relationship, so I came."

Out of money, he went to Belo Horizonte City Hall, where, according to the New York Post, he told a social worker that he had travelled far for love because he was tired of being lonely, and received assistance to return home. Livemint reports that municipal social workers gave him food, access to shower facilities and a bus ticket so he could leave the city.

He has not given up. Livemint quotes him: "I didn't find her, but I'll find another one, and it will be in the same place I went, and I'll go again." IBTimes reports he is staying with a family member and intends to return to Belo Horizonte to find a partner.

## 3. What the account operator said

The operator posted a video acknowledging that the character was artificial. The New York Post reproduces both the original Portuguese and an English rendering. The operator wrote: "I came here to state the obvious: I am made of artificial intelligence. I have never spoken to anyone with this profile. The intention was to gain engagement for sale. I never spoke directly to anyone, much less took anyone to Belo Horizonte. It was a surprise to me to see what is happening."

Livemint quotes the same passage and adds that the operator denied any direct contact with Martins.

The Portuguese version, as the New York Post carries it, is more specific about the business model. The operator wrote that the profile was created to be monetised on TikTok and to sell through the TikTok Shop, and that the form it took was RPA, which is how the operation claims to gain engagement, while insisting that no one had ever spoken to anyone using the profile.

The New York Post reports that the video in which the persona made the invitation was deleted from the TikTok account, which had 300,000 followers.

That is the whole mechanism in one sentence: a synthetic face, a follower count, a deleted pitch, and a real man's savings.

## 4. The market this sits in

The Character.AI filing, made three days before this story broke, shows what the synthetic companion business looks like from the inside of a company that built one at scale.

CNBC reported on October 13 that Character.ai, the startup behind one of the largest AI companion apps, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on Monday, October 12, in the Delaware District bankruptcy court. The company said the platform became costly to develop and grow amid a boom in AI entertainment.

The filings quoted to CNBC read: "In its 4-year lifespan as an active platform, Character has amassed a large and engaged user base. The burgeoning and evolving field of AI entertainment, however, has proven to be costly to develop and grow." And: "These high costs, coupled with potential litigation related liabilities and regulatory engagement have contributed to the Debtor's inability to generate sufficient cash flows to support its business in the present form."

Variety reported that Disney Worldwide Services, a Disney subsidiary, agreed to pay $20 million to become the stalking-horse bidder for a non-exclusive perpetual license of certain Character.ai intellectual property and certain claims. The deal also brings former Character.ai employees to Disney, which had already hired the chatbot provider's former CEO, Karandeep Anand, as chief technology officer the previous month, and they will provide transition services for a limited time to keep the platform operational through the sale.

CNBC reported that Character.ai expects to keep operating with minimal disruptions but that the platform could end up being wound down. It also noted the litigation: in January, the state of Kentucky sued the company, alleging that the platform "preyed on children and led them into self-harm." And it noted the history: in 2024, Google signed a non-exclusive licensing agreement for the startup's language model technology and rehired its co-founders Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, with Shazeer later leaving Google for OpenAI.

Two things are worth separating here. The Kentucky case is about a regulated companion product marketed to a specific audience and is in litigation. The TikTok account in Minas Gerais is an unaccountable account selling access to a fictional farm. They share a business model, not a liability.

## 5. The verdict: false, with context

The claim is false. There is no farm in Minas Gerais, and there is no widow offering a home and financial support to men who come looking. The account operator said so on camera, in the same account, and deleted the invitation.

The context is what the operator's own explanation exposes. This was not a scammer extracting money from a victim one message at a time. It was a monetisation operation: synthetic media built to hold an audience, an audience sold onward through a marketplace, and the returns harvested from engagement. The New York Post reports the operator insisting that no one had ever spoken to anyone using the profile. On the record reviewed here, Martins was not scammed. He was mis-sold.

That distinction matters because the remedy is different. A fraud needs a charge and a victim statement. A business model needs a reader who knows to ask whether the face is real, which is why we look at [what an AI assistant privacy debate actually turns on](/instagram-muse-ai-assistant-privacy-debate/) and at [what these synthetic channels do to the people who build them](/local-llm-vtuber-test/). The same question applies to any feed that monetises intimacy, and it applies to the reader rather than only to the platform.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Did the account operator ever message Martins directly?**
A: No, on the record. Both Livemint and the New York Post carry the operator's denial of any direct contact, and the operator said the intention was to gain engagement for sale.

**Q: What happened to his money and his trip home?**
A: He sold his belongings on October 9 to fund the bus ticket and ran out of money in Belo Horizonte. Livemint reports municipal social workers gave him food, shower access and a bus ticket to leave the city, and IBTimes reports he is staying with a family member.

**Q: Was anyone charged in Brazil?**
A: Nothing in the sources reviewed here reports a charge. No source reviewed here reports a police complaint or an arrest in connection with the account itself.

**Q: Is the Character.AI bankruptcy connected to this case?**
A: No connection has been established. The connection is structural. Both are businesses that monetise synthetic intimacy, and the filing shows the economics of doing that at scale. The Kentucky lawsuit against Character.ai is a separate matter.
