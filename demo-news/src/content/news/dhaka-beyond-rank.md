**Short answer:** The ranking is measuring six things, and only one of them is income. Bangladesh scores low on the two that matter most for the national average, GDP per capita and the perceived corruption that comes with it, and it scores poorly on neither of the two that a visitor actually notices in a street. A cell that says "low" tells you which six columns were thin. It does not tell you what the city is like on a Tuesday morning, and it is not evidence of a place with no working economy.

## 1. What the ranking actually measures

The World Happiness Report builds its country ranking from six inputs rather than from a survey of contentment. The report's own editorial pages set out the method and publish the country table for each edition, and the current edition is the 2026 report.

The six factors are GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy at birth, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption. Two of them are national income measures, one is a mortality statistic, and three are survey-derived.

That composition is the single most useful fact for a reader. A country's position on this list is dominated by income per head and by how corrupt its institutions are judged to be, both of which move slowly and both of which weigh heavily in a national average. Street-level daily experience barely registers.

For the full breakdown of what the ranking measures and what it misses, read our [explainainer on Finland and Bangladesh](/finland-happiest-bangladesh-reality/).

## 2. What Bangladesh scores on the columns that matter

The World Bank's Bangladesh country data page is the checkable version of the same story, and it is where the gap becomes visible. It carries the country's population, GDP and GDP per capita, alongside the World Development Indicators for health, education, trade and poverty.

Two things follow. First, a country can sit near the bottom of a happiness ranking and still have a large working economy measured in GDP per capita rather than in factory output, and the World Bank data is where the composition of that economy shows up. Second, the WHR's corruption and social-support columns are partly derived from survey responses and partly from regional indicators, which is why a country can improve its reported life satisfaction without those inputs moving.

None of this makes the ranking wrong. It makes it a summary of six specific inputs, and a summary is exactly what gets flattened into a shareable map.

## 3. What the cell leaves out

A rank is a national average with a single number attached, and every input in it is an average of averages.

The city-level experience that a visitor or a resident actually reports is not one of the six columns. Employment in the informal economy, the density of family support, whether a river commute is reliable at six in the morning, whether women feel harassed on a bus, and how quickly flood water is cleared after the July rains are all real and all absent from the ranking.

This is the same structural point we make in our [safety maps analysis](/safest-country-maps-footnote/) and our [guide to reading rankings](/how-to-read-rankings/): the rank is a legitimate instrument for the question it was built to answer, and a poor instrument for the question a viral post asks of it.

## 4. How to use this brief

Open the ranking table, read the six inputs, and then look at what they are actually measuring. Note which of the six are thin for the country in question and which are not.

Then read the country's own statistics, not the commentary about them. The World Bank page is the raw table for the economic half. For the survey-derived half, the World Happiness Report publishes its own method notes, and reading them takes ten minutes.

And when you meet a viral "worst country" map, ask the two questions that matter. Which columns drove the position, and who benefits from the map being shared? A visa agent gains from fear. A course seller gains from "worst" tags. That does not make the ranking false. It sets the bar for what you should check before you pass it on.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Does a low happiness rank mean Bangladesh is a poor place to live?**
A: It means the country scores low on the six inputs the ranking uses. It does not measure street safety, employment quality, family support or the pace of daily life, and those are the things a city visitor actually notices.

**Q: Is the ranking just about GDP?**
A: No. GDP per capita is one of six inputs, alongside social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity and perceived corruption. Income and corruption together pull most of the movement in national scores.
